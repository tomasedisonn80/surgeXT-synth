# W01 A01 — Professional scoring sound research

Research date: 2026-11-13 (user timezone). This is an intermediate research note, not a claim of tested bank quality.

## Main finding

The evidence supports recurring *musical functions*, not an industry-wide ranked list of the most-used presets. Original composer interviews repeatedly describe tailoring a limited palette to a particular story, blending synthetic and acoustic identities, and performing/modifying sounds. A new bank can be grounded in these professional practices; none of the sources establishes that Hollywood composers endorse or would definitely use our presets. The 100-per-category allocation is an authoring decision, not a market-share statistic.

## Twelve primary sources and what each supports

1. **Mac Quayle — Spitfire Composer Magazine interview.** https://composer.spitfireaudio.com/en/articles/mac-quayle-discusses-how-eight-hardware-synths-beat-a-thousand-plug-ins
   Direct interview: Pose used analog synth pads and piano melodies; Mr. Robot began electronically and later incorporated strings/woodwinds; American Horror Story: 1984 used a retro synth slasher palette. Design consequence: quiet emotional pads and playable keys deserve as much care as aggressive horror sounds. Web refs: turn744687550view4, turn513228584search3. Full article retrieved.

2. **Mac Quayle — Soundtoys artist interview/case study.** https://www.soundtoys.com/artist/mac-quayle/
   Quayle identifies pulses, drums and drones in the Mr. Robot cue 405.8 Runtime, explaining that delay adds depth, rhythmic emphasis and movement. Design consequence: tempo-aware pulse movement and controllable delay should be musical options, with dry settings available. This is a vendor artist endorsement, not independent prevalence data. Ref: turn744687550view9. Full article retrieved.

3. **Hans Zimmer — Song Exploder, Dune transcript.** https://songexploder.net/transcripts/hans-zimmer-transcript.pdf
   First-person cue breakdown names recurring electronic ostinatos, fully synthesized drum patterns, drones, processed electric cello/guitar, voices and acoustic sources. Design consequence: separate pulse, percussion and atmosphere roles; include restrained and unusual rhythmic designs rather than only conventional trailer impacts. Ref: turn607016764view5. Transcript retrieved.

4. **Kyle Dixon and Michael Stein — original emailed interview, YourClassical.** https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2016/08/15/stranger-things-soundtrack
   The composers describe Prophet-6 tone, ARP-2600 booms/effects using pitch envelopes and FM, and Prophet VS mysterious tones combined with smooth filtering. Design consequence: expressive retro polys, FM/pitch-envelope hits and filtered digital textures are supported use cases. Ref: turn744687550view10. Full article retrieved.

5. **Daniel Hart — The Green Knight interview, Spitfire Composer Magazine.** https://composer.spitfireaudio.com/en/articles/daniel-hart-on-scoring-the-green-knight
   Hart describes using a small set of modified Prophet presets with acoustic instruments, pitched/processed percussion, and a performed ring-modulation change. Design consequence: playable macro transformations and distinct core patches matter more than superficial variation counts. Refs: turn744687550view3, turn381672531view6. Full article retrieved.

6. **Kate Simko and Stacey Lee — Underplayed interview, Spitfire Composer Magazine.** https://composer.spitfireaudio.com/en/articles/underplayed-composer-kate-simko-and-director-stacey-lee-talk-transition-synths-and-collaboration
   Simko describes an electronic palette that crosses club and intimate scenes, rich Moog bass, analog/digital instruments, and a deliberately gritty edge. The director discusses rapid cue transitions. Design consequence: expose density, grit, bass weight and timing controls; support both restrained and foreground settings. Refs: turn744687550view7, turn381672531view5. Full article retrieved.

7. **Ludwig Göransson — first-person score breakdown, Pitchfork video transcript.** https://pitchfork.com/video/watch/critical-breakthroughs-ludwig-goransson
   His Tenet breakdown describes a recorded truck transformed into pitched, side-chained rumbling bass. Design consequence: noise/tonal blends and shaped rhythmic bass are credible synthesis design directions, not a claim to recreate the recorded source. Ref: turn607016764view6. Full transcript retrieved. Source word limit 100 words.

8. **Thomas Newman — British Film Institute interview.** https://www.bfi.org.uk/interviews/scoring-mr-banks-composers-life-hollywood
   Newman discusses glockenspiel and whistle colors for an inner fairy-tale world, smooth dramatic transitions, and creative work with small ensembles and sampled phrases. Design consequence: delicate tuned percussion, clear attacks and controllable releases belong in the bank alongside large sustained timbres. Ref: turn381672531view0. Full article retrieved.

9. **Volker Bertelmann/Hauschka — official collaborative instrument documentation.** https://www.spitfireaudio.com/products/hauschka-composer-toolkit
   The documented prepared-piano palette includes percussive rhythms, bass, atonal plucks, pads and swells, with dry and processed signals. This documents a composer-designed palette offered commercially; it does not establish market penetration. Design consequence: organic-impression synth plucks, resonant metallic tones and separate dry/processed controls. Ref: turn381672531view1. Full product page retrieved.

10. **Paul Haslinger — Arturia artist studio feature.** https://www.arturia.com/stories/paul-haslinger
    The feature describes his hybrid workflow and a demonstrated dark, modulated SEM sound; Haslinger discusses vintage character in virtual instruments. Design consequence: restrained analog-character textures and stable performance controls are legitimate targets. Vendor feature: treat product praise as marketing, not proof of industry dominance. Ref: turn607016764view3. Full article retrieved.

11. **Simon Franglen — Spitfire Composer Conversations.** https://www.spitfireaudio.com/blogs/editorial/composer-conversations-simon-franglen-on-scoring-the-avatar-franchise
    Franglen describes layered synthetic choir colors in Titanic and a deliberate contrast between organic and electronic percussion for Avatar: Fire and Ash. Design consequence: vowel-like synthetic layers and hybrid percussion contrast; do not label synthesized vowels as realistic sampled choir. Refs: turn607016764view4, turn381672531view2. Full article retrieved.

12. **Jörg Hüttner — Arturia interview.** https://www.arturia.com/stories/jorg-huttner
    Hüttner explains dirty sequences for drive, odd-meter/longer patterns to avoid repetition, time-synced modulation, and subtle anxious sounds for documentary scenes. Design consequence: variable pulse density, asymmetric pattern options and subtle tension controls. Ref: turn381672531view3. Full article retrieved.

## Suggested 10-category implementation map (design inference)

- 100 emotional/sustained pads: warm, airy, glassy, dark, neutral, string-like, soft vintage.
- 100 drones and atmospheres: quiet suspense, tonal foundation, ambiguous/noisy layers, breathing motion.
- 100 pulses and sequences: eighth/sixteenth/dotted/triplet, asymmetric patterns, bass and upper-register variants with actual timbral differences.
- 100 basses: round fundamental, articulated analog, gritty hybrid, sub/upper harmonic balance, pulsed and sustained roles.
- 100 leads and expressive solo synths: soft lyrical, reed-like, portamento, brassy, digital, expressive pressure/vibrato.
- 100 keys and plucks: intimate struck envelopes, synthetic keys, muted/bright plucks, clean and prepared-impression.
- 100 mallets and bells: tuned resonances, glass, wood-like, metallic, short and long decays; harmonic and deliberately inharmonic variants labeled clearly.
- 100 hybrid ensemble colors: synthetic strings/brass/vowels/winds, not claims of orchestral sample realism.
- 100 percussion and impacts: tuned drums, clicks, noise percussion, metallic strikes, pitch-envelope hits, restrained punctuation as well as large accents.
- 100 transitions and designed effects: rises, falls, swells, reversed-envelope impressions, tension gestures, booms and motion beds with timing notes.

## Requirements inferred from the interviews

Every preset needs a clear role and playable range, with enough headroom for layered scoring. Prefer modulation controls that change emotional intensity, brightness, motion, density and space without breaking tuning or causing uncontrolled level spikes. Make dry/short settings available for dialogue scenes and articulated music. Avoid fixed musical phrases that prevent adapting the sound to picture. Flag intentionally atonal sounds rather than promising every patch works harmonically everywhere. Avoid copying film themes, proprietary soundbank content, or composer/brand naming that implies endorsement.

A genre-flexible *collection* is achievable as a design objective; any individual preset cannot be objectively guaranteed suitable for every genre or every cue. Genre utility and Hollywood suitability ultimately require human audition, musical context and composer feedback.
